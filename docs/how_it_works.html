1

Collect examples: other people's breakfasts

A model learns from examples. Nobody has filmed thousands of stir-fry dinners, so we use a public set of breakfast videos called Breakfast Actions. Cutting fruit and cutting vegetables look much the same to a camera.

The 8 actions the model learns

Real frames from the training videos. People watched every video and wrote down which action happens at each moment. We grouped their 48 labels into these 8.

What a labeled video looks like

A 3½-minute video of a person making fruit salad. The colored bar shows the label for each moment.

Keep some people back for a fair test

The dataset has 52 people. We split them into groups. A person in the test group never appears in training. So a good test score means the model works on new people. It did not just memorize the people it saw.

Each kitchen was filmed by several cameras at once. We also kept one camera position, webcam01, out of training completely. The hardest test, T2, uses new people and this unseen camera.

The same moment from two cameras in one kitchen.

2

Turn video into numbers

To a computer, an image is only a grid of numbers: one brightness value per color per pixel. One short clip is more than a million numbers. Most of them, like the wall color, say nothing about the action. So we first shrink each clip into a short, useful description.

The name for this list: an "embedding"An embedding is a short list of numbers that describes something. Here it describes 1 second of motion. The encoder makes one embedding every 2.1 seconds of video. We made all of them once, for every video (1 h 43 min on the Mac), and saved them. Training then reads these small lists and never touches the video again.
3

Train the head: the part that decides

The encoder describes the motion. It does not know our 8 actions. The head is a second, much smaller network on top of the encoder. It reads the description and decides: cut, pour, stir, and so on.

What is a "head"?

Encoder · 7.9 million weightsDescribes motion in general. Borrowed and frozen.
Head · 0.66 million weights
Decides which of our 8 actions it is. Trained by us.

Drawn to scale by number of weights. We train only the blue part: 8% of the model.

A model is a chain of steps. The data enters at one end, and the answer comes out at the other end. The last part of the chain, where the answer comes out, is called the head. The earlier part that does the general work is the encoder, also called the backbone.

Why train only the head?

Option A

Train the whole model ourselves

  • Needs hundreds of thousands of videos. We have 549 training videos.
  • Needs days on large GPUs.
  • With so few examples, it memorizes the training people and fails on new ones.
✗ Not possible here
Option B · what we did

Borrow the encoder, train only the head

  • The encoder already learned motion from 240,000 videos.
  • The head only has to learn one small thing: which motion pattern means which of our 8 actions.
  • Trains in 38 seconds on the Mac.
✓ This is called transfer learning

Why a small head?

📉
Little data35,629 examples is small. A big head would memorize them instead of learning the actions.
🧠
The hard part is doneThe encoder already does the difficult work. The head only maps 1,024 numbers to 8 scores.
⏱️
Fast to train38 seconds per training run. So we could test many settings in one afternoon.
🔋
Fast on the Jetson0.1 ms per decision on the device, so almost all the time goes to the encoder.

The head looks at 17 seconds, not 1

One embedding alone can be unclear. Is the hand reaching for a knife or for a bowl? So the head reads 8 embeddings in a row, about 17 seconds of video, and decides about the moment in the middle (yellow).

How training adjusts the weights

  1. Guess. The head looks at 512 examples and gives its scores. At the start, the weights are random, so the guesses are random.
  2. Check. Compare each guess with the label a person wrote. The loss is a single number for how wrong the guesses were.
  3. Find the direction. For every weight, compute whether a small increase or a small decrease would lower the loss.
  4. Adjust. Move every weight a small step in that direction. Then repeat with the next 512 examples.

One pass through all 35,629 examples is called an epoch. We ran 10 epochs: 700 rounds of guess, check, and adjust.

Try it: adjust one weight

The curve shows how wrong the model is for each value of one weight. The goal is the bottom. Try a step size of 0.1 (slow), 1.0 (one jump) and 2.2 (it jumps too far and gets worse). Real training does this for 658,184 weights at the same time.

Word check: "training", not "calibration"

Setting the weights is called training or fitting. Calibration means something else in machine learning: making the scores honest, so that "70%" is right 70% of the time. We did not calibrate the model.

4

Package the model for the Jetson

We trained on the Mac with PyTorch. The Jetson does not have PyTorch. So the model travels as a standard file, and the Jetson converts that file into a fast program for its own chip.

PyTorch modelOn the Mac. PyTorch is the software we used to build and train it.
→
ONNX file (34 MB)A standard file format for models. Any software that reads ONNX can run it. We checked: same answers as PyTorch.
→
TensorRT engineOn the Jetson. TensorRT rebuilds the model for this exact chip. The result runs only on this type of chip.

Fewer digits, more speed: FP32 and FP16

Every weight is stored as a number with a fixed size. FP32 uses 32 bits and keeps about 7 digits. FP16 uses 16 bits and keeps about 3. FP16 numbers are half the size, so the chip moves half the data, and it has special circuits for FP16 math. The cost: each number is rounded more.

The same value, stored two ways
A ruler zoomed in near 0.4167. Every tick is a value the format can store exactly. A number between ticks is rounded to the nearest tick.

Why the rounding changed no answers
FP16 moved each score by at most 0.006. For every moment, this chart shows the gap between the top score and the second score. An answer can flip only if that gap is tiny.

Most answers win by a wide gap. Only 42 moments were close enough to possibly flip, and none of them did. On a different model with many close calls, FP16 could change answers. That is why we test it instead of assuming.

Say it in one breath

"I trained a small action-recognition head on top of a frozen video encoder, using only public data. I moved it to a Jetson Orin Nano as ONNX and compiled it with TensorRT in FP16. It runs in 7 milliseconds per decision against a 2-second budget. Then I checked on the device that all 6,179 test answers matched the training machine, and ran the whole chain, from video to planner, in real time at 4% of the time budget."
